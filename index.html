Message from your instructor

Building a tree in four steps

  1. Pick traits and score each organism: 0 = ancestral state, 1 = derived state. The outgroup (most distant relative) shows the ancestral states.
  2. Find the trait shared by the most organisms. Those organisms form the largest clade; mark that trait with a tick on the branch.
  3. Inside that clade, repeat with the next trait, so groups nest inside groups.
  4. Every split (node) has two branches. Organisms that share the newest node are sister taxa.

Part 1: Understanding cladograms

A cladogram is a branching diagram with organisms (taxa) at the tips. Each branch is a clade. A node is where two clades split from a common ancestor, and the two clades that split there are sister clades. Tick marks show where a new trait evolved; every organism after that tick inherited it.

Ancestral traits are shared by everyone in the tree, so they say little about closest relatives. Derived traits shared by a subgroup reveal who shares a more recent ancestor.

Homology and homoplasy

If two taxa share a trait, the simplest assumption is that it evolved once in their common ancestor (homology, like hair in humans and dogs). Sometimes a trait evolves independently more than once, like wings in insects, bats and birds. This is homoplasy (convergent evolution). A trait that switches back to the ancestral state is a reversal. When your tree needs the same trait to appear twice, you may have found homoplasy.

Monophyletic, paraphyletic and polyphyletic

Modern biology only recognizes monophyletic groups: a common ancestor plus all its descendants. A paraphyletic group has a common ancestor but leaves out some descendants. A polyphyletic group lumps together organisms that did not inherit their shared traits from a common ancestor (for example, whales and seals are both aquatic mammals with reduced limbs, but evolved those features separately).
